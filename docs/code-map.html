Who makes the rules that a Waterville code inspector enforces

A Waterville building permit uses rules from four sources. The city writes ordinances. The State of Maine writes statutes and rules. Private organizations write model codes. The state also publishes training manuals. This page shows who writes each source, how much legal force it has, and when the inspector uses it.

What is the ICC

Most technical building rules in Maine come from one private organization. This section explains that organization and how its books become law.

International Code Council

The ICC is a private nonprofit organization in the United States. It is not a government agency. It writes model codes. A model code is a complete set of building rules that a government can adopt.

Building officials, builders, engineers and manufacturers help write the codes. The ICC publishes a new edition every three years.

The ICC holds the copyright for the text. Anyone can read the codes free of charge in the ICC online viewer. The ICC sells the right to copy, search or print the text.

The ICC also owns General Code. General Code operates eCode360, the website that shows the Waterville City Code.

How an ICC book becomes Maine law

  1. The ICC writes the model codes. Examples: the International Residential Code (IRC) for houses, the International Building Code (IBC) for other buildings, and the International Energy Conservation Code (IECC) for insulation and energy use.
  2. The Legislature gives the authority. A statute tells the state to adopt one building code for all of Maine. The code applies in each town with more than 4,000 people (10 M.R.S. § 9724).
  3. The state adopts the codes by rule. The rule is the Maine Uniform Building and Energy Code (MUBEC). It names the 2021 ICC editions and lists the Maine changes. These editions apply from April 7, 2025.
  4. The city enforces the code. Waterville has about 15,800 people, so MUBEC applies. Chapter 127 of the City Code makes the inspector responsible for it.
  5. The inspector applies the text. The inspector reads the ICC book with the Maine changes. The inspector cannot change the rules.

Order of authority

When two sources do not agree, the higher source controls. A city ordinance can add local rules. It cannot conflict with state law. A training manual explains the law. A manual is not law.

State law
Maine statutes. The Legislature passes them. They give the inspector powers, set the penalties and tell when the building code applies.
30-A M.R.S. § 4452: enforcement and civil penalties. 10 M.R.S. § 9724: the building code applies in towns with more than 4,000 people.
State rules
Agency rules. State agencies adopt them under a statute. The building code rule adopts the ICC model codes. This makes the ICC text part of Maine law.
MUBEC (16-642 CMR, now listed as 08-003 CMR), septic systems (10-144 CMR ch. 241), shoreland zoning (06-096 CMR ch. 1000).
Adopted codes
Model codes and standards. The 2021 IRC, IBC, IEBC, IECC and IMC, NFPA 101, and the plumbing and electrical codes.
They have legal force only in the edition that the state rule names, with the Maine changes.
City law
The City Charter and the City Code. The City Council adopts them. They cover zoning, property maintenance, rental registration, floodplains and local building rules.
§ 275-4.26: fences. § 205-7: property maintenance notices. § 127-3: permit fees.
Procedure
Court rules and city forms. Court rules tell how a case goes through court. Forms tell an applicant what to submit.
M.R. Civ. P. 80K: land use citations. Rule 80B: appeals. Building Permit Application. HHE-211 plumbing form.
Advisory
Guidance manuals. The state training program for inspectors publishes them. They explain how to do the work. Some are old. They are not law.
Legal Issues and Enforcement Techniques Manual (2017). Shoreland Zoning Manual (2008).

Court decisions are not on this ladder. Courts interpret statutes and ordinances. A decision of the Maine Supreme Judicial Court applies to all towns. Example: Mills v. Eliot, 2008 ME 134, tells how to write findings of fact for a permit decision.

Who is involved

Select a level to show fewer cards. Each card tells what that organization makes or decides.

Show

City of Waterville

City Council

Adopts and changes ordinances and fee schedules. Gives staff permission to take enforcement cases to court.

Makes: City Code chapters, new laws, attachments

Code Enforcement Office

Three staff: Director Dan Bradstreet, Code Enforcement Officer Adam Bradstreet and Ordinance Compliance Officer Todd Buckmore. The office issues permits, does inspections, answers questions and enforces the code.

Makes: permits, certificates of occupancy, notices of violation, forms

Planning Board

Reviews site plans, subdivisions and shoreland applications. The City Planner supports the board. The Code Director reports on approval conditions.

Decides: site plan, subdivision and shoreland approvals

Board of Zoning Appeals

Hears appeals of inspector decisions. An appeal must be filed within 30 days. The board makes a new decision on the full facts. The board also gives variances.

Decides: appeals, variances (§ 275-6.2)

Fire Department

Does the life safety review for building permits. Keeps the rental registry. Inspects for business licenses and short-term rentals.

Makes: life safety reviews, the rental registry

City Solicitor

The lawyer for the city. Handles court cases. Answers legal questions that the inspector cannot answer alone.

Makes: legal opinions, court filings

Electrical and plumbing inspectors

The Municipal Electrical Inspector checks work on one- and two-family houses (§ 127-9). The Local Plumbing Inspector issues plumbing and septic permits. The city does not publish who holds these positions.

Makes: electrical and plumbing permits, inspections

State of Maine

Maine Legislature

Writes the statutes in the Maine Revised Statutes (M.R.S.). Title 30-A sets the duties, powers and penalties of the inspector.

Makes: statutes

Maine Office of Community Affairs (MOCA)

Trains and certifies code enforcement officers. This program was in the Department of Economic and Community Development (DECD) before. MOCA publishes the building code rules, the certification rules and the training manuals.

Makes: MUBEC and certification rules, guidance manuals

State Fire Marshal

Enforces the Life Safety Code (NFPA 101) for the state. Issues construction and accessibility permits for many commercial and multifamily buildings.

Makes: state fire and accessibility permits

Department of Environmental Protection (DEP)

Writes the shoreland zoning rules. Each town ordinance must agree with these rules. Reviews work near protected natural resources.

Makes: 06-096 CMR ch. 1000, NRPA permits

Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS)

Writes the rule for septic systems. The rule controls septic design, the HHE-200 form and the septic work of the plumbing inspector.

Makes: 10-144 CMR ch. 241

Licensing boards

The plumbing and electrical licensing boards adopt the plumbing code and the National Electrical Code. Local inspectors apply these codes.

Makes: plumbing and electrical code rules

Courts

District Court

Hears land use citations under Rule 80K. This is how the city gets civil penalties. A certified inspector with permission can present the case for the city.

Decides: violations, penalties, orders

Superior Court

Hears appeals of decisions by the Board of Zoning Appeals and the Planning Board under Rule 80B.

Decides: appeals of board decisions

Maine Supreme Judicial Court

Writes the Maine Rules of Civil Procedure. Decides appeals. Its decisions apply to all towns.

Makes: court rules, case law

Publishers and vendors

International Code Council (ICC)

A private nonprofit organization. It writes the model building codes: IRC, IBC, IEBC, IECC and IMC. Maine adopts the 2021 editions by rule. The ICC holds the copyright. See "What is the ICC" above.

Makes: model building codes

General Code (eCode360)

Shows the official City Code online at ecode360.com/WA3904. The ICC owns General Code since 2017. The eCode360 terms of use limit copies of the text.

Shows: the City Code online

NFPA

The National Fire Protection Association. It writes NFPA 101, the Life Safety Code, and NFPA 70, the National Electrical Code.

Makes: fire and electrical standards

IAPMO, ASHRAE, ASTM

IAPMO writes the Uniform Plumbing Code. ASHRAE writes standards for ventilation and energy (62.1, 62.2, 90.1). ASTM writes test standards, for example E1465 for radon.

Makes: standards that the codes refer to

Federal

FEMA

The Federal Emergency Management Agency. It makes the flood hazard maps and operates the National Flood Insurance Program. Chapter 145 (floodplains) of the City Code follows this program. The inspector is the city Floodplain Administrator.

Makes: flood insurance rate maps

The largest gap. The model codes contain the technical rules that the inspector checks on site. Examples are stair dimensions, guard heights, fire separations and insulation values. The inspector's workflow

A permit starts with a first question and ends with a certificate of occupancy. A complaint starts with a report. It can end with a notice of violation or a case in District Court. Each step shows the sources, the other people, and the preview tool that helps.

Questions from the public

People call or come to the office. They ask if they need a permit, what they can build and how far from the property line. The city has no online permit system. Applications are PDF forms.

Sources

Ch. 275 zoning tables, Ch. 127 building, city forms

Other people

Residents, contractors, lawyers and title searchers who ask about open permits

Preview: public answers with permit checklists, permit guide, fee estimate

Plan review and approvals

The inspector checks the zone, setbacks, lot coverage and site plan. The inspector also finds which other approvals are necessary first.

Sources

MUBEC and the ICC codes, NFPA 101, Ch. 275, Ch. 145 floodplains, Ch. 161 historic districts, Ch. 244 site plans, state rules

Other people

Planning Board, Fire Department life safety review, plumbing inspector, State Fire Marshal, DEP

Preview: project approvals checklist, research desk

Permit and fee

The inspector issues and signs the permit. If work starts without a permit, the fee is double, to a maximum of $500. After 30 days the maximum is $1,000 (§ 127-3B).

Sources

§ 127-3, fee schedules, the fees on the form

Other people

Electrical and plumbing inspectors for their own permits

Preview: fee estimate from published fees only

Site inspections

There are a minimum of three: foundation, framing before it is covered, and final. A missed inspection call can cost $100 per day. Most of the week is used for this step.

Sources

The ICC codes and standards. On site, the inspector uses memory or the ICC viewer.

Other people

Builders, certified third-party inspectors (§ 127-5B)

Preview: saved sections can be read without a connection. The tool cannot do inspections.

Certificate of occupancy

The inspector issues it when the building agrees with MUBEC and the Life Safety Code (§ 127-6). The office keeps permit records for the life of the building.

Sources

§§ 127-5, 127-6, NFPA 101

Other people

Fire Department

Preview: case notebook for the property

Complaint

Examples are poor property maintenance, unregistered vehicles and unsafe buildings. A resident calls or sends an email to make an appointment. The resident then completes a paper form.

Sources

Ch. 205 property maintenance, § 210-10 orders to leave a building, 17 M.R.S. dangerous buildings

Other people

Fire Department, residents

Preview: printable complaint sheet, case notebook

Notice of violation

This is a written order. It gives the section, the facts, the necessary correction, a deadline, the right to appeal and the penalty range. For Ch. 205, a copy also goes to the mortgage holder.

Sources

§ 205-7, § 275-6.1, 30-A M.R.S. §§ 2691 and 4452, the Legal Issues Manual

Other people

City Solicitor, for the words of the notice

Preview: notice drafter with Word export, deadline calculator

Agreement or court

The city and the owner can sign a consent agreement. If not, the inspector files a Rule 80K land use citation in District Court. The owner is found from the registry of deeds. A certified copy of the ordinance goes with the citation.

Sources

M.R. Civ. P. 80K, the 80K Manual, 30-A M.R.S. § 4452(3) penalty levels

Other people

City Council permission, City Solicitor, District Court

Preview: court citation packet checklist

Appeals. A person can appeal any inspector decision to the Board of Zoning Appeals within 30 days. The board makes a new decision on the full facts. A person can then appeal to Superior Court under Rule 80B. For each appeal, the inspector takes the $25 fee and sets the hearing within 45 days. The inspector also sends notice to owners within 300 feet a minimum of 14 days before the hearing (§ 275-6.2).
